If you believe in backing founders, you can back storytellers too. This series shares how you can angel invest in the arts and the creation of new worlds.

A companion piece to Part I: Angel Investing in Film

In my last post, I wrote about why some of us in the tech world are investing in independent documentary films: the production timelines, creative updates, and exit models can feel surprisingly familiar to startup/tech investing. There’s another corner of the creative world that also fits neatly into this portfolio mindset: Broadway.

This past year, I had the grand experience of investing in Suffs, a musical about the women’s suffrage movement written by Shaina Taub. It opened at the Music Box Theatre, and earlier this year, we found out it was nominated for six Tony Awards and won two. A pinch-me moment, to say the least for my first Broadway investment.

What made it even more special was that I invested through Nothing Ventured Productions (NVP), a Broadway investment collective founded by my college friend Kathryn Minshew, along with Alexandra Cavoulacos and Christina Wallace, all successful tech entrepreneurs who brought the angel investing playbook into theater production.

Kathryn and I go way back to our days at Duke where we hilariously took a ‘wilderness skills’ class together. She’s also known for founding The Muse, a career platform used by over 70 million people to research companies and careers. Now she’s extending that entrepreneurial DNA to the world of live performance, and bringing her friends along for the ride.

NVP is building a portfolio of Broadway projects, just like an angel syndicate might invest in a batch of seed-stage startups. They use a roll-up vehicle model to pool checks from accredited investors, often with lower minimums than a traditional Broadway investment would require. It’s democratizing access to a historically closed world.

Why Broadway?

Unlike film or tech, Broadway shows offer relatively clear and immediate cash flow visibility. You know exactly how many tickets are sold, what the merchandise numbers look like, and what’s coming in the door week by week. To me that transparency is rare and frankly refreshing. Here’s how one recent NVP investor update broke it down:

Wrap = ticket sales value that day
Gross = ticket value from attendees that day
Advance = value of tickets sold for future performances
Groups not yet paid = confirmed group sales pending cash receipt

You can think of these metrics a bit like MRR or bookings in a SaaS business, signals of demand and health, plus the added bonus of nightly applause and regular critical theater reviews.

Broadway as an asset class

Like film, Broadway offers a spectrum of opportunities across genre and scale. Here’s a quick primer:

  • Original Musicals (like Suffs): high risk, high reward, often mission-driven
  • Revival Musicals: more predictable, often tied to nostalgia or recognizable IP
  • Plays: generally lower budgets, but sometimes smaller commercial upside (similar to musicals that new plays are higher risk/reward, establish plays lower risk/reward)
  • Limited Engagements/Touring: opportunities to recoup via short runs or roadshows

For the investors the terms are generally equity-style participation; some offer recoupment waterfalls and royalty participation, plus the occasional invite to Suffs-related parties and performances.

The Suffs Model

From a capital perspective, Suffs followed a fairly standard budget structure for a Broadway musical of this scale, raising just around $19 million to get to opening night (NYTimes). That included “hard costs” (like set construction, cast and crew salaries, rehearsal space, and administrative overhead) as well as reserves for previews, marketing, and contingencies.

Once a show opens, Broadway becomes a weekly cash flow business. Broadway musicals operate with a recurring cost structure that typically ranges from $400K-$1M per week, depending on the scale of the show, the venue, and marketing needs. That includes everything from salaries and rentals to advertising and theater expenses. You can think of it like a startup’s monthly burn, and break-even is tied to consistently hitting a certain occupancy threshold at premium ticket pricing.

For investors, this opens up a unique kind of financial visibility. You’re not waiting years for a liquidity event. You see gross ticket sales every week, along with real-time metrics on advance sales (future demand), merchandise revenue (non-ticket upside), and even line-item profitability estimates. Some investor updates even include “recoupment scenario” charts showing weeks to break even at various levels of capacity (e.g. 70%, 80%, 90% full houses), much like a SaaS model might show customer acquisition cost payback or time to profitability.

Of course, recoupment isn’t guaranteed. But with strong press, savvy pricing strategy, and energetic audience response, the upside can move quickly and in the case of Suffs, momentum only grew post-opening though it ended its Broadway run earlier than we had hoped. It’s now shifting to a National Tour later this fall.

As with angel investing, risk is real. Many shows never recoup. But there are also scenarios, especially with strong press, merchandise traction (Suffs sold thousands of merch opening night), and big-name backers (the Suffs producers include Hillary Clinton and Malala Yousafzai), where things can tip from break-even to upside.

The investor experience

One of the most fun parts of being involved with Suffs was the community from early access to dress rehearsals, to special merch collabs, to getting a behind-the-scenes look at how the show was evolving in previews. Investor updates included real-time box office strategy (“flash sales” to drive demand), press updates (e.g., New York Times, The New Yorker, Playbill), and performance recaps. The investor comms read like a blend of a portfolio company update plus a fan letter.

When I finally saw the show live with friends, it was electric. Here’s a photo of me holding the program in the Music Box Theatre. We were beaming. Felt special to watch it knowing we were some of the financial backers to make this story possible.

Closing curtain

If you’re a tech investor with a love of storytelling, community, and creative exposure, Broadway might be your next unexpected asset class. Your investment might not always win a Tony, but you might just help bring something beautiful into the world, and learn a new world along the way.

Organizations like Nothing Ventured Productions are making Broadway investing more accessible, and there are other groups and individual producers creating similar opportunities for those interested in supporting live theater. Drop them a line if you’re interested in upcoming shows.

Note: This is not investment advice. This is me on sabbatical, having fun with the time I am on this planet, supporting people doing brave things and telling stories that I’m excited to see in the world. I get zero financial benefits from sharing this post, except that perhaps more of my friends will start supporting the arts if they didn’t know it was within reach.
