If you believe in backing founders, you can back storytellers too. This series shares how you can angel invest in the arts and the creation of new worlds.

In the summer of 2021, Linda Xie tweeted a Mirror.xyz link to crowdfund a new documentary that she was co-producing named Vitalik: An Ethereum Story, a character-driven documentary film on the challenges and rewards of building a new world.

In just over 48 hours, the film team raised $2 million worth of Ethereum for the production of the documentary. Some donors received producer credits and NFTs created by digital artist pplpleaser. On April 15, 2025, the film premiered on Apple TV and Amazon Prime Video.

I was inspired by this exposure because historically my only alternative investments had been in VC-backed start-ups and I didn’t realize film was an asset class I could participate in.

After Linda posted the 2021 tweet, I went to Sundance and met Jenny 8 Lee, documentary producer who runs Goodside, a community of film lovers who convene at festivals like Sundance, Cannes, and SXSW. Part of what I love about entrepreneurship is not only building new products, but also building communities and (I hope!) improving the way societies exist and evolve. Perhaps most importantly, the evolution of any new world originates with storytelling.

As a newcomer to the world of investing in the arts, I wanted to share my journey so if other folks in tech wanted to jump in, you don’t have to start from zero.

So far, I’ve invested in a documentary via a grant mechanism, and I’m evaluating an equity or grant investment in a second film. I’ve also made a grant into a short story platform that’s used as lead gen for filmmakers.

For context: I’m the former co-founder and CEO of HumanFirst, acquired by ICON plc (NASDAQ: ICLR) earlier this year, and in a past life I worked at KKR, a private equity firm. Over the past 10+ years, I’ve made 65+ angel investments– mostly small checks in start-up companies, as well as in a few VC and PE funds.

At a high level, there are two types of independent films: narrative and documentary.

Independent films and those developed directly by distributors (like Apple, HBO, or Netflix) follow very different funding and production models. Unlike studio films, indie films typically don’t have distribution secured at the outset. That said, a streamer or distributor may acquire or license an independent film later in its lifecycle. In fact, that’s often the goal.

Case Study: Two documentaries I’m supporting

The introductions to both documentaries were organic. Linda introduced me to Carrie Weprin, who produced the Vitalik film. Then I met Jessie Seng, when she was matched as my roommate at Sundance last year in the Goodside house.

Decision point: equity or grant investments

When investing in film, you can choose to give grants or invest for equity (or do a blend).

  • Grants are typically made to a 501(c)(3) fiscal sponsor. Your support is philanthropic. These are often tax-deductible and aligned with impact goals. You can use a Donor-Advised Fund (DAF) to facilitate this.
  • Equity means you’re treated as an investor in the film’s LLC. If the film makes money, you get a return based on a waterfall structure (after the film recoups production costs).

For example the *holds you tight* team is raising both:

  • Equity Investments for individuals looking for financial return. Investors are entitled to recoup their principal investment plus a premium and a share in the film’s backend profits. The *holds you tight* filmmakers are capping the amount of equity raised to maximize the potential for return on investment (ROI). Equity investments start at $50k.
  • Grants & Donations (via Women Make Movies as fiscal sponsor). Grants and donations receive preferential credit placement to equity investors. (Update on June 5, 2025: You can make a grant directly via their Tribeca Film Festival landing page.)

Communication & investor updates

The investor updates are what I’ve enjoyed the most. In start-ups, investor updates are bullet-pointed, objective, and often formulaic:

  • Revenue
  • Product shipped
  • Customers won/lost
  • What we need help with

But in film, the updates are creative expressions themselves.

Jessie Seng, director of FTNL, sent one of the most moving early updates I’ve received. She shared not just logistical updates, but also the emotional journey of what it means to fundraise, create, and stay independent as a first-time indie filmmaker after having big production experience at HBO Max. Her email mixed reflections about Cambodian American history being “not a fit” for many traditional funders, the cost of staying independent to center survivors’ stories instead of sensationalizing perpetrators, and small victories- like receiving a grant after filming a powerful interview with a survivor.

Jessie’s updates are raw, emotional, and deeply personal… a reminder that investing in an independent documentary film is often about supporting a creator’s lived experience, not just a final product.

As another example, Carrie Weprin’s updates for *holds you tight* are structured, polished, and production-driven. Her update included:

  • A detailed phase-by-phase budget and timeline overview (development, production, post-production)
  • Clear fundraising targets tied to each phase
  • Different contribution tiers (starting at $5,000 for “Special Thanks” credit, with higher tiers offering Co-Executive Producer credits)
  • A planned cadence of quarterly updates with milestones (shooting, editing, grant outcomes, festival submissions)

Carrie’s approach reflects operational rigor almost like a seed-stage start-up update, while maintaining a creative and mission-driven heartbeat. Her materials made it easier for me to onboard to the terminology and expectations of film development. That’s part of my goal: I hope that by financially supporting these projects, I’m also creating a playground to learn a new industry.

All to say, both styles are brilliant. Understanding the filmmaker’s approach will help you set your expectations as an arts investor and also give you a view into what stage you’d want to start participating in.

So far with respect to timing, I have chosen to support film projects in the early stages. Investing is not my full time job, so I don’t have the capacity to do much diligence. My first checks are small — and often grants. I look for teams that will share regular investor updates, similar to what I get with my angel VC-backed portfolio companies.

If the filmmakers are strong communicators and share regular updates, I may switch from grant support to equity investments later as the filmmakers have further developed the IP and as startup people like to say: ‘derisked’.

Timelines can vary dramatically — 18 to 36+ months is not uncommon for docs.

A changing landscape and cuts to public arts funding

Another important context to understand: traditional funding sources for independent film, like grants from the National Endowment for the Humanities (NEH), are facing significant and historic budget cuts. For filmmakers like Jessie Seng, this had a direct impact, as her previously-awarded California Humanities grant was canceled a few weeks ago.

Jessie and Kitty shared that as NEH and similar federal institutions reduce their arts funding, independent filmmakers are increasingly having to look for non-traditional funders, like individuals, private foundations, and alternative models including direct angel investments (could be me and you!).

This creates both a challenge and an opportunity. The challenge is obvious: fewer institutional grants means more competition and more instability for critical storytelling projects. But the opportunity is that individuals like us, who may have historically focused only on start-ups, can now step in to support creators who might otherwise be left out of traditional funding pipelines.

Supporting films like FTNL and *holds you tight* isn’t just about backing a project. it’s about keeping critical, human stories alive in a shrinking public arts landscape.

Lesson: ask for what you want

Just like in VC, you don’t get what you don’t ask for. The same applies here. If there’s a certain participation strategy or amount you’d like to invest… just ask.
